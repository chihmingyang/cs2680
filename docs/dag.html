How this figure was built

What a node is

  • The unit is what a reader holds in their head, not what a lecture covers. A concept is a mental model: A mental model: a way the system behaves that you hold in your head and that stays true across products (the loop, the context, the KV cache, Amdahl's ceiling). Concepts form the skeleton of the DAG and never depend on practices.
  • A practice applies concepts: A decision rule, technique or worked case that applies concepts (grep first, checkpoints and limits, FlashAttention, the Caveman case). Practices depend on concepts and on other practices.
  • The test for a concept: state it without naming a product, a lecture or this course. If the sentence only works with “Claude Code” or “CLAUDE.md” in it, the node is the general idea and the product is its example (CLAUDE.md and auto memory are one concept, persistent text; skills and offloading are one concept, keep it out until needed; subagents and multi-agent systems are one concept, agents share only text).
  • One node, one claim: it can be written as a single sentence, it has its own prerequisites, and removing it makes some later node unreadable. Worked cases from the lectures (Caveman, Superpowers, dsh-superagent) are not nodes; they are examples inside the node they illustrate.
  • Every chapter of every lecture article maps onto at least one node, so nothing taught is lost. Catalog chapters (lookup lists) are not concepts and stay out:
    • L1 ch.10: Catalog chapter: nine questions, ten properties, course scope — a lookup list, not a concept
    • L5 ch.9: Catalog chapter: eight architectures, communication options, two protocols — lookup
    • L6 ch.5: Catalog chapter: 26 run failures, one table per layer — lookup
    • L6b ch.7: Catalog chapter: eight memory failures, seven design questions, systems and metrics — lookup
    • L7 ch.10: Catalog chapter: eight benchmarks, five goal levels — lookup
    • L8 ch.10: Catalog chapter: eight harnesses, papers, system-level techniques — lookup
    • L9 ch.10: Catalog chapter: notation, formulas, other architecture directions — lookup
    • L11 ch.10: Catalog chapter: ten pitfalls, numbers from three assignments — lookup

What an edge is

  • A → B means “understand A before reading B”. B's “why” sentence must say what becomes unreadable without A.
  • Only direct prerequisites are drawn. If A → C follows from A → B → C, it is not drawn.
  • Edges look only at ideas, never at lectures. Prerequisites that live in a later lecture are therefore expected; they are exactly the gap this figure is meant to expose.
  • A concept never depends on a practice. If it seems to, either the edge points the wrong way or the node is misclassified. tools/dag.py --check rejects it.

How difficulty is assigned

  • BasicCan be stated in one sentence and understood from everyday experience or one prerequisite; without it, most of what follows cannot be read. 20 concepts.
  • IntermediateNeeds two or more prerequisites held at once; usually a design decision or a cost calculation. 61 concepts.
  • ExpertGoes inside the mechanism (formulas, hardware units, algorithms) or judges the validity of research results; skippable if you do not build serving, harnesses or evals yourself, because upstream judgments do not depend on it. 12 concepts.

Difficulty is a property of the concept, not of its depth in the figure. One hard rule: a prerequisite can never be harder than the concept that depends on it (Basic ≤ Intermediate ≤ Expert). A violation means either the edge or the difficulty is wrong, and tools/dag.py --check rejects it.
